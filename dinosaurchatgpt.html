🦴 Dinosaur Fossils

Dinosaur fossils are more than just bones. Scientists have also discovered fossilized skin, scales, feathers, organs, and other soft tissues. These discoveries give scientists clues about what dinosaurs looked like and how their bodies worked.

Some fossils have even preserved evidence of dinosaur colors. Scientists have studied tiny structures called melanosomes, which can help reveal the colors of feathers and scales.

Scientists have also discovered fossils containing parts of internal organs. These rare discoveries help scientists understand dinosaurs in much greater detail.

🌋 The Beginning of Dinosaurs

Dinosaurs first appeared during the Triassic Period, roughly 230 million years ago. They evolved from earlier archosaur ancestors and were initially small animals, many of which walked on two legs.

At first, dinosaurs were not the dominant animals on Earth. They lived alongside many other reptiles and early animals. Over millions of years, dinosaurs became more successful and diversified into many different groups.

🌎 Dinosaurs Spread Around the World

During the early part of the dinosaur age, Earth's continents were joined together as a giant landmass called Pangaea. This allowed animals to spread across large areas.

As Pangaea slowly broke apart, dinosaur groups in different parts of the world began to evolve in different ways.

North America, South America, Asia, Africa, Europe, and Australia each developed their own unique dinosaur communities.

🌸 The Cretaceous Period

During the Cretaceous Period, dinosaurs became extremely diverse. Tyrannosaurs, ceratopsians, hadrosaurs, ankylosaurs, and many other groups lived across the planet.

Flowering plants also became increasingly common during this time, changing the environments where dinosaurs lived and providing new food sources for herbivores.

☄️ The End of the Dinosaurs

About 66 million years ago, a major extinction event occurred at the end of the Cretaceous Period. Most dinosaur groups disappeared.

However, birds survived. Modern birds are descendants of theropod dinosaurs. In this sense, dinosaurs are not completely gone—they still live among us today in the form of birds.
