Our Solar System

A Vast Cosmic Neighborhood

Our solar system is made up of the Sun, eight planets, five officially recognized dwarf planets, hundreds of moons, and thousands of asteroids and comets.

It is located in the Milky Way, a barred spiral galaxy. The Sun lies in a smaller section of the galaxy called the Orion Arm, or Orion Spur. Our solar system travels around the center of the Milky Way at about 515,000 miles per hour. Even at that incredible speed, it takes roughly 230 million years to complete one trip around the galaxy.

Why Is It Called the Solar System?

The name solar system comes from the Latin word solis, meaning Sun. The word "solar" refers to anything connected to our Sun, which is the central star of our planetary system.

Could There Be Life Beyond Earth?

So far, Earth is the only world where we know life exists. However, scientists continue to explore our solar system and planets around other stars in the search for signs of life.

Discovering life beyond Earth would help scientists understand how common life might be throughout the universe.

The Outer Reaches of the Solar System

The solar system extends far beyond the planets. Past Neptune lies the Kuiper Belt, a region filled with countless icy objects. Pluto, one of the best-known dwarf planets, is located in this distant region.

Farther away is the Oort Cloud, a huge spherical shell of icy objects surrounding the solar system. Scientists have never directly observed the Oort Cloud, but its existence is supported by mathematical models and observations of comets that may come from this distant region.

The Oort Cloud may extend as far as 1.6 light-years from the Sun. One astronomical unit, or AU, is the average distance between Earth and the Sun, about 93 million miles.

The Sun's Protective Bubble

The Sun constantly releases a stream of electrically charged particles called the solar wind. This creates a huge bubble around the solar system called the heliosphere.

The solar wind eventually encounters material between the stars and slows dramatically. This boundary is called the termination shock, located roughly 80 to 100 astronomical units from the Sun.

The Voyager spacecraft have traveled beyond this boundary. Voyager 1 crossed it in 2004, while Voyager 2 crossed it in 2007. Voyager 1 entered interstellar space in 2012, followed by Voyager 2 in 2018.

Moons Across the Solar System

Hundreds of moons orbit planets, dwarf planets, and even asteroids throughout our solar system. Among the eight planets, Mercury and Venus are the only planets without moons.

The gas giants Jupiter and Saturn have especially large collections of moons. Their systems of moons can almost resemble miniature planetary systems.

Even the dwarf planet Pluto has five known moons. Its largest moon, Charon, is so large compared with Pluto that their gravitational interaction causes Pluto to wobble as they orbit each other.

How Did the Solar System Form?

The solar system formed approximately 4.6 billion years ago from a massive cloud of gas and dust. The cloud collapsed, possibly after being disturbed by a nearby exploding star called a supernova.

As the material collapsed, it formed a rotating disk known as the solar nebula. Gravity pulled more and more material toward the center.

Eventually, the pressure and temperature became high enough for hydrogen atoms to fuse together and form helium. This nuclear reaction released enormous amounts of energy and marked the birth of the Sun.

Farther from the center, pieces of dust and rock collided and joined together. Over time, these objects became larger and eventually formed planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids, and comets.

The Four Rocky Planets

The planets closest to the Sun are Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars. They are known as terrestrial planets because they have solid, rocky surfaces.

When the solar system was young, the intense heat near the Sun prevented many lighter materials from remaining there. As a result, the inner planets became relatively small and rocky.

The Giant Planets

Farther from the Sun, temperatures were cold enough for gases and icy materials to remain. Gravity gathered these materials into the large outer planets.

Jupiter and Saturn became gas giants, while Uranus and Neptune became ice giants. Together, these four enormous planets form the outer planetary region of our solar system.
